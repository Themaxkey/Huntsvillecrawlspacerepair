## Why Crawl Spaces Fail in the Tennessee Valley

Huntsville sits in a humid subtropical pocket with long, heavy summers. From June to September the outside air routinely carries a dew point in the low seventies, which is the number that matters. When that air is invited into a crawl space through foundation vents, it meets ground and framing sitting nearer sixty-five degrees, and it does the only thing physics allows: it gives up its moisture on the coldest surface it can find.

That surface is usually the underside of your floor.

This is the part homeowners find hardest to believe, and it is the most important thing on this page. **Crawl space vents were required by code for decades, and in this climate they make the problem worse rather than better.** They were conceived for drier regions. Here, opening a crawl space to July air is a way of pumping water into the structure of the house.

The result builds slowly. Wood moisture content climbs past twenty percent, which is the threshold where decay fungi become active. Fiberglass insulation absorbs damp, gets heavy, loses its R-value and eventually falls out of the joist bays altogether. Ductwork sweats. Floors above turn cold and springy. And because roughly half the air you breathe upstairs was in the crawl space first, the smell follows.

## Limestone Country Behaves Differently

North Alabama sits on Mississippian limestone, and that changes what happens to water once it reaches the ground.

Limestone dissolves. Over long periods, water moving through it opens joints and solution channels, which is why this region has caves, springs and the occasional sinkhole. For a homeowner it means groundwater does not always behave the way it would in the heavy clay further south. Water can arrive at a crawl space through a route that has nothing to do with the surface grading, and it can leave just as suddenly, which is why a crawl space here can be bone dry in one visit and have standing water in the next.

There is clay too, sitting in a layer over the rock, and it does the usual shrink and swell through wet winters and dry Augusts. But the mistake is assuming Huntsville behaves like Birmingham. It does not, and a contractor who treats it that way will fix the wrong thing.

## Where the Damage Shows Up First

Floors that flex or slope, usually noticed near the middle of a room rather than at the walls.

A musty smell that is strongest on the ground floor and worse in summer.

Insulation lying on the crawl space floor instead of between the joists.

Doors in the older parts of town that stick in August and swing freely in February.

Condensation on ductwork, or air conditioning that never quite keeps up because the ducts are losing their cooling to a damp cavity.

## What an Inspection Actually Involves

Somebody goes under the house. That sounds obvious and it is regularly skipped.

A proper assessment measures wood moisture content rather than guessing at it, finds where water is entering, checks whether existing supports are doing anything, looks at the state of the ductwork and insulation, and reads the grading and downspouts outside — because a surprising share of crawl space water problems are solved above ground, with a downspout extension and forty dollars of dirt.
