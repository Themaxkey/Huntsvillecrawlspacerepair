Basements are the minority in north Alabama — most houses here sit on crawl spaces or slabs. But Huntsville's terrain produces a particular kind of basement, and a particular kind of water problem to go with it.

## The Huntsville Basement

Where the land slopes, which around Monte Sano, Blossomwood and the older hillside neighborhoods it frequently does, houses were built with daylight or walkout basements cut into the grade. One or two walls are fully below ground, holding back saturated soil, while another opens to daylight.

That asymmetry is the problem. The buried side takes hydrostatic pressure from groundwater and the exposed side does not, so the wall loading is uneven, and water arrives at the join between the two.

## How Water Gets In

**Through the wall-floor joint**, the cove where the slab meets the wall. This is the commonest entry point and it is a pressure problem, not a crack problem.

**Through cracks in a poured wall**, often vertical and often at a form tie.

**Through the block itself.** Hollow block walls fill with water in the cores and weep through the face. Efflorescence — the white powdery deposit — is the mineral left behind when that water evaporates, and it tells you water has been moving through the wall.

**Over the top of the foundation** where grading slopes back toward the house.

## Interior Versus Exterior

Exterior waterproofing means excavating around the foundation, sealing the outside face and installing drainage at footing level. It addresses water before it reaches the wall and it is the more thorough approach. It is also disruptive and expensive, and on a hillside lot with mature planting it is sometimes impractical.

Interior drainage takes water that has already reached the wall and gives it a controlled route out: a channel around the perimeter inside the footing, feeding a sump. It does not stop water touching the wall, but it stops it reaching the room, and it is far less invasive.

For most Huntsville basements, interior drainage with a properly sized sump is the sensible answer, with exterior work reserved for cases where the wall itself is failing.

## Bowing Walls Are a Different Job

A wall that is bowing inward is not a waterproofing problem, it is a structural one — soil pressure is exceeding what the wall can carry. Put a straightedge against it: if the middle sits further into the room than the top and bottom, it needs carbon fiber straps, wall anchors or bracing, and it needs assessing before anybody talks about drainage.

Waterproofing a bowing wall without stabilizing it is treating the symptom of the thing that will eventually be expensive.
