New Market sits in the northeast corner of Madison County against the hills, and the ground here holds and moves water in ways that matter under a house.

## Springs and Seeps

This part of the county is limestone country with a long history of springs. Where water emerges from rock it does not announce itself politely — it can surface in a low corner of a lot, or under a house, with no relationship to where rain fell.

A crawl space fed by a seep behaves differently from one fed by runoff. It stays wet in dry weather, it does not respond much to downspout corrections, and it needs drainage designed to carry a continuous small flow rather than an occasional large one.

Identifying which you have is the whole job. It is also why we would rather look at a New Market property after a dry fortnight than after a storm — persistent water in dry conditions tells you something runoff never would.

## The Housing

Predominantly older and rural, much of it pier-and-beam on tall crawl spaces, with a scattering of newer builds on subdivided land.

The older houses have generally had a long time to accumulate damage, and what we find is decay concentrated where the damp has been most persistent rather than evenly across the framing. That is workable — targeted repair rather than wholesale replacement — provided somebody assesses it properly rather than pricing by square footage.

## Ground Movement

The clay layer over the limestone does the usual seasonal shrink and swell. Occasionally there is more localized settlement where the rock beneath has a soft, soil-filled seam. That is uncommon and it would be wrong to suggest otherwise, but it produces sharper, more concentrated cracking than clay movement does, and it is worth distinguishing.

## The Work

Find the water and characterize it — runoff, seep, or condensation. Drain accordingly. Repair what has decayed. Seal, line and dehumidify.
